The pitch circle — overtones, wrapped at the octave

log(frequency) mod log(2). One revolution = one octave. Each chord tone draws an inward comb — one tick per partial.

Reading the circle. Each tick is one partial of one chord tone. Tick length and opacity shrink with partial number (lower = louder = more perceptually salient). The warm radial bands behind the ticks mark every angle where two or more voices share a partial; their width grows with the strength of the lock-in (lower partial numbers = louder coincidence = wider band). Bands tagged ×3 are triple coincidences where all three voices line up.

The colours. root, third (M3 or m3), fifth. The 12 grey reference ticks around the outside mark equal-tempered semitone positions, labelled with interval names.

What to notice in major (4 : 5 : 6). All three colours converge densely near R, M3, P5, and M7. The lowest shared partial between root and fifth is the 3rd (loud); between root and third is the 5th. Everything locks in early.

What to notice in minor (10 : 12 : 15). The m3's fundamental (~94°) is isolated — no other voice's low partials reach it. The first triple-coincidence (all three colours at one angle) is up at the 6th partial of the m3, vs. the 3rd for major. The chord still locks, but higher up where the partials are quieter.
