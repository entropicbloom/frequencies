# The Intrinsic Structure of Chords

*Draft. Working write-up for the question that drives the pitch-circle diagram.*

## Intro

- Why do major and minor chords sound so reliably cheerful or sad?
- Play a major and a minor chord to anyone, ask which sounded happier, and they'll reliably pick the major.
    - (Cultural variation likely exists; assume for now there's something more than convention.)
- The math definition of the two chords is easy: they're built from specific frequency ratios.
- So given three frequencies, we can compute the ratios and classify them as a major or minor triad (conditioned on knowing it's one of the two).
- But this mathematical definition gives us no insight into why the two are perceived so asymmetrically on an emotional level.
- It seems arbitrary at first, which is why the cultural-prior explanation is attractive.
- The question I want to ask: could there be something inherent in the mathematical structure of a major or minor triad that gives a clue about the emotional character of hearing them?
- If we take the definition to be just the ratio of three real numbers, probably not.
- But we have good reasons to move to a richer mathematical structure.

## Background

- Introduce overtones, and why they co-occur with the fundamental in the real world.
- Objection: why would this be relevant if pure sine-wave chords also have clear major/minor characters?
- Because perception doesn't happen without the brain. Under a predictive-processing view of what the brain does, it likely models the overtone structure of a frequency even when it's absent from the audio.
