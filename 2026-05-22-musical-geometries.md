# Musical geometries

*2026-05-22*

An exploration of geometric and structural representations of musical intervals, chords, and scales — and the question of whether any such structure can capture the felt difference between major and minor.

## What's in here

Each file is a self-contained HTML document — open it in a browser. All share the same visual aesthetic (paper-ink-rule palette, Cormorant Garamond + DM Sans).

### Fretboard

- **`pentatonic-patterns.html`** — guitar fretboard diagrams for four pentatonic box patterns (minor & major), with interval labels. The reference / starting point of the conversation.

### Circle diagrams

12 chromatic positions arranged on a clock face. Each chord or scale is the polygon formed by its notes, root pinned at 12 o'clock.

- **`scale-circles-labeled.html`** — minor/major chord, minor/major pentatonic, natural minor, major scale. Interval labels (R, ♭3, 4, …) outside the ring.
- **`scale-circles-blank.html`** — same six diagrams, no labels. Pure shape.

### Harmonic series

- **`harmonic-alignment.html`** — vertical ladder of harmonics 1–16 of a virtual fundamental, with two columns showing where the major triad (harmonics 4, 5, 6) and minor triad (harmonics 10, 12, 15) live. Both **linear (frequency)** and **logarithmic (pitch)** versions side by side. Each harmonic is labeled with its pitch-class interval.
- **`harmonic-overlap.html`** — for each chord, the three notes' own overtone series shown on a shared frequency axis. Filled dots = the overtone lands on an integer harmonic of the root (reinforcement). Hollow dots = it falls between (foreign frequency, near-miss beating). Vertical bars above mark coincidences (where 2+ chord notes' harmonics meet).

## The arc of the conversation

We started with the fretboard. The question was whether a "minor vs major" interval visualization could be designed cleanly with root on top. That moved into chromatic-circle diagrams of chords and scales — five-, seven-, three-note polygons with the root pinned at 12 o'clock.

Then a deeper question: **is there a mathematical object whose geometry actually captures the felt difference between major and minor**, rather than encoding it via a convention like "root on top"?

A few attempts and what each one revealed:

1. **Color palettes from a chromatic-to-hue mapping.** Dead end. Without anchoring the root to a fixed hue, modes of the same scale would have the same palette and minor/major could rotate into each other. Even with the anchor, the choice of which hue is "home" is arbitrary — synaesthetic, not structural.

2. **Tonnetz / Riemannian inversion.** Better than the chromatic circle in that major and minor become up- vs down-pointing triangles. But still a symmetric structure with an automorphism that swaps them — same triangle, mirrored. The picture *labels* the distinction; it doesn't *contain* it.

3. **Harmonic series alignment (4 : 5 : 6 vs 10 : 12 : 15).** A genuine asymmetry, and one that no transformation can undo. The major triad sits on three consecutive low integers; the minor triad on three non-consecutive higher ones. The integers don't have a symmetry that swaps these. This was the first structurally non-arbitrary distinction.

4. **Overlap of the three chord notes' own harmonic series.** Each note in a played chord produces its full overtone stack. Major triads have many places where the three stacks coincide (six points in the first ten harmonics, including 7.5 as an M3-P5 coincidence). Minor triads have fewer (three points; one is a triple at h6).

   This is what produces consonance in rich-timbre instruments. **But:** pure sine waves have no overtones, and major-vs-minor *still* differs somewhat to listeners on pure sines (less strongly, but present). So the overlap story is **a contributor, not the necessary cause**.

5. **The integer-lattice / Cayley-graph view.** The deepest abstract version. The multiplicative group of positive rationals (mod octave) is free abelian, generated by the primes:

   - prime 2 → octave
   - prime 3 → perfect fifth
   - prime 5 → major third
   - prime 7 → harmonic seventh
   - …

   Pitches sit at integer-coordinate points in an infinite-dimensional lattice — one axis per prime. Edges = multiplication by a prime. Distance in the lattice tracks consonance (Tenney height). The (3, 5)-plane projection of this lattice is the Tonnetz; major and minor occupy distinct triangles there.

   This is "geometry" in the structural sense — relational, not pictorial. It's plausibly the structure the brain internalizes as a **harmonic-series template** (the mechanism behind virtual / residue pitch). Major matches the template cleanly; minor matches it awkwardly, with the implied fundamental sitting far below the chord root. This is what Rameau called the *fundamental bass* and Riemann formalized as the dualism of major and minor.

## Where it lands

A picture-geometry of pitch (chromatic circle, Tonnetz) can't *generate* the major/minor distinction, because such structures have automorphisms that swap them. A **ratio-lattice geometry**, generated by the primes under multiplication, has no such symmetry — major and minor live at genuinely distinct addresses. The integer lattice is the candidate for what makes them different *as structures*.

How the brain accesses that structure: multiple transducers act on top of it.

- Harmonic overlap (rich timbres only)
- Critical-band roughness in the cochlea
- Combination tones / nonlinear hearing
- Virtual-pitch / harmonic-template matching
- Learned cultural priors

Removing any one of these (e.g., using pure sines to remove overlap) weakens but does not eliminate the perceived distinction — which is consistent with the picture that the *structure* is geometric, and the *physical mechanisms* are just different ways of being sensitive to that structure.

Whether the *qualia* — the felt brightness vs shadow — reduces to template-fit or requires something more is the part this conversation doesn't settle.
